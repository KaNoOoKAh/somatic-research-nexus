The release of ink by octopus and squid is commonly understood as a defensive response that distracts predators and allows escape. However, I propose an additional possibility centered on the organism's somatic and neural state during the act of ink release.

During a predatory encounter, an octopus may be simultaneously preparing multiple survival strategies, including camouflage, rapid color change, body deformation, flight, or direct defense. In this brief interval, neural activity throughout the animal's body is likely operating at an extremely heightened level as sensory information, motor responses, and environmental assessments occur simultaneously.

I hypothesize that the shape and appearance of some ink releases may reflect this intense state of bodily organization. Rather than functioning solely as a cloud of pigment, the expelled ink may momentarily resemble the form of another octopus because it emerges during a period when the animal's entire somatic system is rapidly coordinating movement, shape, and appearance.

Under this view, the ink cloud could be interpreted as a defensive chemical release but as an involuntary projection of the organism's active body image, generated at the peak of survival-related neural and somatic activity.
